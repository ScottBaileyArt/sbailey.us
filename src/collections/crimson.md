exhibition_statement:
- 'Scott Bailey: Crimson Lucy Till'
- 'It is impossible not to become immersed in the sheer theatricality of Scott Bailey''s paintings
  and to search for significance or meaning in the imagery he creates. The subject is, as
  much as anything, substance itself - Scott explores the material possibilities of paint,
  the character and life it contains - but there is also an aspect of performance in the making
  of the works. As with any good drama, there are contradictions and dichotomies running throughout
  his work: intention vs. spontaneity, surface vs. depth, motion vs. seizure.'
- Foremostly these are action paintings, with an obvious precedent in the drip paintings of
  Jackson Pollock, but Scott has an intention when he begins a painting, and then relinquishes
  control of its outcome and allows the paint to take its own form in response to his gesture.
  With great thoroughness, he prepares each colour to a specific viscosity, applies it and
  then maneuvers the surface of the canvas (tipping, tilting, folding, scrunching it), so
  the paint responds - through gravity, through pressure, through friction - in its own unpredictable
  way to the action. The most recent work, still in progress, is a series of canvases each
  a mere 30 cm wide, but 3 metres tall, on which he has poured his paint from the top, and
  then allowed gravity to run its course. Thus Scott's paintings are at once both an experiment
  with and an homage to the materiality of pigment suspended in a medium.
- In many of the recent paintings, Scott works directly onto the unstretched canvas, and has
  exploited the qualities of the folded or crumpled cloth, so that the paint follows the material's
  topography. Scott is exploring the theatrical possibilities of fabric, exploiting the dynamic
  rhythms of folds - the ridges and crevices, the shadows and highlights - and the resulting
  paintings frequently appear to reproduce these qualities. There is an obvious precedent
  in baroque religious painting, by artists such as Rubens and Caravaggio, where luxuriant,
  often red, swooshes and swathes of fabric, which have an integral theatricality of their
  own, frame a dramatic scene, often a nativity or a death.
- All things remind us, as Nietzsche pointed out, that sometimes depth can hide on the surface
  of things. There is a tension in Scott's paintings between surface and representation -
  the painting becomes a space where surface and depth shift. Viewers have always seen imagery
  of underlying structures, the strata under the surface - previously, analogies were made
  with geological formations, and now, with his predominantly crimson palette, something very
  visceral can be found in his work - flesh, muscle, fat, bone. In many of the works, and
  especially the tall paintings, there is a particular impression of fissures - deep slashes,
  cracks or wounds - and that the surface is rupturing. (There are echoes of Barnett Newman
  - like Pollock one of the great American abstract expressionist painters - but Scott's works
  are the messy jagged, raw versions of Newman's precise zip paintings, where pristine fields
  of colour are scored by single vertical lines, but the surface is never breached.)
- 'The artist has been measurably impacted by his experience of Cairo , especially in his
  awareness of the physical and immediate nature of life - and death. There is a certain explicitness
  in the sight of meat hanging in the open shop-fronts (in comparison to its hermetically
  sealed packaging in Western-style supermarkets), or the carnal rituals of the feast. Scott
  has also experienced one of the seminal life experiences whilst in Cairo - the birth of
  his children: an everyday miracle, the physical materialization of flesh from nothing. Commonplace
  dramas and scenarios - birth, butchery - resonate in Scott''s vivid panels. These flat surfaces
  remind us of the physical nature of the world and especially of the body - this extraordinary
  machine that we inhabit so unthinkingly, which we use and move in so insolently.'
- 'And here lies one of the inherent paradoxes in Scott''s work: there is a pervading association
  with action - explicit and implicit, in its creation and in its composition, yet this work
  captures and freezes action. The paint responds to Scott''s gesture, but the painting is
  an inert remnant of that moment of movement; the surface suggests motion and rhythm yet
  remains static. And this contradiction Scott probes to an extreme in his series of small
  sculptures which record the permutations of a drop of liquid as it splashes on a surface.
  This split-second and most mundane transformation Scott has painstakingly dissected and
  recreated by molding, carving (then polishing and painting) cheap modeling plastic, to create
  these precious-looking objects, like stars or coronets, again red, but here desirably shiny.'
- By conceiving, exploring, playing with, and ultimately accepting the inherent dichotomies
  which co-exist in existence, Scott reminds us to appreciate the stuff from which we and
  our world are made, which so often we take for granted or even find repellant, and reveals
  the profound value and beauty in the physical matter which we inhabit and in which we are
  contained.
- Lucy Till is an independent curator currently living and working in Cairo
- Facilis Descensus Averno? Adrian Saunders
- At first sight these canvasses challenge. The deep ruts of imperial purple, the glowing
  shades of phoenician crimson and the rippled textures of porphyry create an image of sensual
  and inviting luxury that draws the viewer into a close dialogue with the painter. These
  are no mere explorations of colour, but rather a series of carefully thought-out pieces
  that describe a concept that is at once profound and redolent of the poetic and mystic truth
  that surrounds us, if only we had eyes to see. For Bailey is a painter of ideas and of a
  more spiritual cast of mind, that leads him toward a web of connections both of motif and
  idea that may at first seem unexpected, but which after reflection appear increasingly eloquent
  and of lucid clarity.
- There is possibly a key here that allows us to glimpse the workings of Bailey's ordered,
  but still fruitful universe. He is a perceptive painter, in a period when painting may seem
  passé. But for Bailey, painting is a means of setting-up resonances that allow him to articulate
  an essentially intellectual and utterly coherent vision of the varied visual stimuli that
  crowd in upon us from every side. He is a man on a journey, downwards and upwards, and it
  is of both inner and exterior enquiry.
- So, "facilis descensus Averno ." When Virgil's Aeneas asked the way to the Underworld, he
  was given the somewhat chilling reply, "The way down is easy." The full stop bespeaks a
  certain finality. But like the Roman hero, Bailey does find a way out and with the generosity
  of the seeker asks us to share it. Resonance and association allow a contemplation of beauty
  in the unexpected, which leaves a feeling of peaceful optimism and a sense of the journey
  accomplished. These canvasses ask the viewer to contemplate what many may choose to avoid.
  Are we right to detect the ripple of muscle, or the gross structure of tendon and sinew?
  A whiff of the shambles? Perhaps more. Perhaps we are made to look even deeper and asked
  to contemplate. Are we brought as low as can be in order to start the inevitable and astonishingly
  fecund journey back to the light? Is it then, when in the stillness that we start to look
  deeper into these pieces, that they start to take on a life of their own?
- Expressed with eloquent, almost baroque brushstrokes, and drips of paint falling in dramatic,
  sometimes calligraphic swirls, the rich pompeian reds seem somehow less bloody; stalactites
  possibly, rippling across the wall of a ruby cavern, or the draped brocades and silks so
  beloved of Titian, Caravaggio or Rubens. A hieratic grandeur and a sense of exuberant theatricality
  are manifest. The whole begins to resonate to that subtle music of simile and luxurious
  metaphor, found in light and delicious, tactile colour. There are pieces that draw the eye
  downwards, those that bring the eye to rest on a corner or a well-found detail, those that
  overwhelm. Bailey is always eloquent and his rhetoric demands attention. But it is deeper.
  He writes his text with confidence and in doing so, leads us to look into the beauty of
  the familiar. He is never sordid. Shocking maybe, but always measured and tightly controlled.
  He sees a fascinating and fertile complexity in simplicity and then suggests that we open
  that inner eye that leads...where?
- Adrian Saunders teaches Art History at the American University in Cairo
