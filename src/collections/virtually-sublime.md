legacy_prefix: c10
statement: Virtually Sublime questions whether it is possible to achieve the painter’s notion
  of the sublime in the landscape through contemporary technology, utilizing satellite imaging
  as inspiration for paintings that are repeatedly fragmented and re-assembled through digital
  mapping and painting processes. The installation includes a 16 foot tall and 53 foot wide
  painting of a virtual (derived from satellite images) landscape of Mt. Rainier. A second
  piece involves a live projection of a painting of a satellite image of Mt. Rainier onto
  a white, scale model of the mountain.
legacy_statement_page: c1b.htm
exhibition_statement:
- Virtually Sublime I remain agnostic whether it is possible today for a painting to hold
  the power of the sublime as was claimed by romantic landscape painters like Caspar David
  Friedrich, J.M.W. Turner, Albert Bierstadt, and those of the Hudson River School , or professed
  by more recent abstract artists such as Marc Rothko, Barnett Newman, Yves Klein, and James
  Turrell. I have come even to wonder whether recent generations are capable of experiencing
  what they called the sublime, or whether that emotion is even culturally relevant.
- Web-based satellite image servers such as Google Earth® now allow us to access and understand
  the landscape in new and engaging ways. From ergonomic office chairs, we can click and tap
  our way through digital perspectives that Lewis and Clark could not have conceived of. Much
  has been gained with this impressive technology to be sure, but I am also left with the
  sense that something has been lost.
- As the earth is surveyed and recorded in increasingly greater detail, I wonder if we may
  be developing a cynical hubris—the notion that we know and have conquered the landscape
  to the degree that we are immune to the complex sensation of the sublime's “delightful horror,”
  or “terrible joy.” When we look at a mountain range today, without sentiments of the Manifest
  Destiny Americans once felt or fear of the boundless untamed wilderness, can we experience
  Kant's “terrifying sublime” and any of its accompanying awe, veneration, pride, hope, ecstasy,
  dread and melancholy?
- The sublime was believed present not only in the mountains, but in the canvas and paint
  of the artist's creations. For several 1950s New York artists, abstract shapes and drips
  of paint could hold that transcendental force without representation of the landscape, and
  viewers confirmed it by claiming to be moved to tears standing in front of their works.
  Here, recognizable images of the mountains were unnecessary, supplanted by the atmospheric
  colors and formal qualities of the canvasses.
- This exhibition is my most sincere attempt at connecting with that mysterious spiritual
  power of nature through paint and contemporary technology and through the iconic and powerful
  image of Mount Rainier . It is my hope that the cinematic scale of this installation and
  the quintessentially human and loving touch of the brush elicit feelings that can be called
  at least virtually sublime.
- In the magnets of computers will be stored
- Blend of sunset over wheat fields. Low thunder of gazelle. Light, sweet wind on high ground.
  Vacuum stillness spreading from A thick snowfall.
- Men will sit in rooms Upon the smooth, scrubbed earth Or stand in tunnels on the moon And
  instruct themselves in how it was. Nothing will be lost. Nothing will be lost.
- Virtually Sublime, 2006 Acrylic, Oil, and Enamel on Canvas, Tyvek, Wood, and Plexiglass
- Recent computer mapping technology has allowed me to lift my view directly above this gallery
  about 6,000 feet, facing in the direction of the painting, and I fly, as the satellite flies,
  60 miles almost due west across the Cascade Curtain. I have used this technology to remotely
  study Mount Rainier , an icon of power and timelessness, in extraordinary detail and from
  impossible perspectives. Digital images ironically already abstract and painterly were collected,
  cut, and pasted into a grid, then recreated with quotations of Modernist painting conventions.
  After having been fragmented through the mapping, digital, and painting processes, individually
  composed abstract paintings are hung salon-style on the wall so that they come together
  again to express the mountain on both the micro and macro scales. This piece is at once
  miniscule in comparison to the real mountain, extremely large by painting standards, and
  still not large enough to fit on the wall.
- Above Rainier, 2006 Extruded Polystyrene, Celluclay, Latex, Acrylic, Canvas, Projection
- This piece mirrors the process of satellite mapping servers, which take flat digitized overhead
  satellite images and bend them over a virtual topographical scaffolding of corresponding
  points of longitude, latitude, and altitude. In this way, though it is based on calibrated
  measurements and can provide extremely precise visual information, the image is computer
  generated and not real. The Virtually Sublime painting is based on images achieved through
  this process.
